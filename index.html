Strategy that comes from
actually doing the work.

I studied mechanical engineering at Colorado State, but somewhere between thermodynamics and structural analysis, I realized I cared more about the "why" than the "how." Why does this product exist? Who needs it? What problem does it actually solve? That instinct pulled me toward product — and it's never let go.

Coming out of engineering school, I joined PwC and then Accenture, where I spent years embedded with product management teams helping them think through how to innovate and how to sustain growth through major strategic transitions. The MBA at Georgetown sharpened the business rigor. But both chapters — before and after Georgetown — were fundamentally about consulting. I loved the work. What I longed for was skin in the game.

So I crossed to the other side of the desk. At Gogo, GCI, CableLabs, and Calix, I stopped advising and started owning — roadmaps, P&L, team performance, market outcomes. Then at FreeWave, everything I had learned came together. I took a 30-year-old hardware company and helped it find genuine product-market fit with a new customer base, a new product-led operating model, and a new place in the market. Rebuilding the revenue model, the go-to-market motion, and the product architecture all at once — that's the work I've realized I'm exceptionally good at.

Across all of it, I've led diverse, cross-functional teams and global delivery organizations spanning four continents — building the kind of alignment, collaboration, and shared momentum that turns a strategy on a slide into outcomes your customers actually feel.

The Problem

Most pivots don't fail because
the strategy is wrong.

They fail because the organization isn't rebuilt to execute it. The CEO sees the destination clearly. The board has approved the direction. Maybe there's even a prototype or early customers. But underneath the surface, the entire operational engine of the company — sales compensation, marketing messaging, support workflows, development priorities, even the org chart itself — is still optimized for the old business.

Leadership teams find themselves in an agonizing middle ground: too committed to turn back, but unable to accelerate because every function is pulling from a different playbook. The result is a slow, expensive bleed of resources, morale, and market timing.

What makes this particularly painful is the isolation. CEOs and CPOs navigating these transitions rarely have peers inside the organization who fully grasp the scope of what needs to change. Board members want progress updates, not vulnerability. Direct reports are simultaneously managing the old business and trying to build the new one, often with conflicting incentives.

The Transition Audit breaks that pattern — bringing structured diagnostic rigor, cross-functional strategy development, and the pattern recognition of someone who has led this exact transformation before.

Notes from the field

Does this sound familiar?

Your delivery velocity is accelerating. Your users' problems are not getting solved.

I keep hearing and reading the same thing across AI-assisted product work: we are shipping the wrong things faster. Shipping a capability and a user realizing a benefit are two separate events, and agents only accelerated the first.

Brian Stahlhammer  ·  Boulder, CO  ·  5 min read

In this post, I give you:

  • A two question test that sorts any piece of work on your roadmap into what an agent can own and what stays with your people.
  • The reason new products fail that test by default, and the one metric that gets them out.
  • Three questions to take into your next product review, and the one sentence that gives your PMs cover to push back.

Every good product team I know is already using AI to prototype concepts, run competitive analysis, and compress development cycles. That capability is real, and most of you should be pushing it harder than you are. It will still never do the part of this job that decides whether a product works, because building products for humans requires humans, and the only question worth arguing about now is which activities sit on which side of that line.

Give the tools their due first

The boundary only means something if you are honest about how far out it sits.

An agent will build a working prototype from a description in an afternoon. It will read every competitor release note from the last two years and tell you what changed. It will turn a pile of interview transcripts into themes faster than your best analyst. It will write production code against a clear spec, run the tests, and fix what it broke.

Every one of those has the same property. You can check the answer. The prototype runs or it does not. The competitor shipped the feature or they did not. The tests pass or they fail.

That property is the whole story.

Where it stops

Does the operator trust the alarm at 2am, or wait for the phone call she actually believes? Does the field tech stop calling support? Does the buyer understand what she just signed? Will anyone change how they work because of what you shipped?

Nothing checks those answers. No test, no dashboard, no agent. A person has to use the thing, or watch somebody use it, and then decide.

I spent twenty years shipping to exactly those users. Operators on an alarm panel. Technicians up a tower in weather. Passengers at 35,000 feet with one shot at connecting before they give up. You do not learn whether that software worked by querying it. You learn by watching a face.

An agent cannot tell the two categories apart. Point one at either and it returns something that looks finished.

Agent autonomy is capped by outcome verifiability.

How an outcome gets judged is a property of the work. You do not get a vote. How much the agent owns is your decision, every sprint. Let the second outrun the first and you have bought speed with no way to know it helped.

Try it on your own work

Two questions set the ceiling

Take something your team is building right now. In under a minute you will know how much of it you can safely hand an agent, and what has to stay with a person. Only the second question is yours to decide.

Velocity trap

A core workflow redesigned straight from a ticket

Nobody on the team has watched the current workflow being used, so the agent optimizes the description of the problem instead of the problem. It will look finished and it will ship.

New products fall into the trap by default

Everything above assumes you have instruments. On an existing product you usually do: a funnel, a baseline, telemetry, a support queue with history.

Start something new and none of that exists.

No baseline. No cohort. No trend to break. The first real usage data lands months after the decisions that matter. Every outcome that counts is a judged outcome, which drops the entire effort into the right column of that grid before anyone writes a line of code.

Now add agents. Velocity feels free, because nothing can contradict you yet.

AI has made it cheap to ship products fast. It hasn't made it easier to ship products that matter. Dave Scott, Product at a Distance

That is where most AI-accelerated work goes wrong. Not incremental features. New bets, built fast, judged by nothing until launch.

Customer Benefit Metrics move the work

Dave Scott's fix is a Customer Benefit Metric, and it is the sharpest tool I have found for this problem.

A CBM measures the benefit your customer receives. Not the benefit to your company. It is the only instrument I know of that sits on the adoption side of the release.

Revenue, attach rate and NPS are business metrics. MAU, traffic and funnel dropout are usage metrics. Both are real and both look backward. Scott calls them the rear-view mirror. A CBM is the windshield.

For Amazon, NPS is not a CBM. DAU is not a CBM. Dollars saved per order is.

Retention is not one either, which is what trips up teams who assume outcomes over outputs already covers this. Retention tells you somebody stayed. It never tells you what they got.

Write one before you build and a judged outcome becomes a measurable one. The alarm workflow stops being "operators should trust it" and becomes time from alarm to action, or the share of alarms acted on without a confirming phone call. Now the agent has something to build against and the reviewer has something to check.

That is the move. Not less velocity. A better definition of done.

What you hand over, and what you do not

Most AI guidance lists approved tools. That is the easy half.

Before an agent touches the work, produce two things.

The agent gets a definition of done a machine can check, anchored on the CBM rather than on the ticket. Tests, contracts, the benefit metric, the guardrails. If you cannot write it, the work is not ready to delegate. Pointing an agent at a paragraph of intent is a wish with a deadline.

The human keeps the judgment no test replaces, with a name and a date on it. Not "we will validate with users." A person, a week, and what she will look at.

Scott puts that on the calendar as a toll booth: a standing weekly review aimed at opportunity definition and concept work, not sprint demos. If your only recurring product review is a sprint demo, the judged outcomes never get judged.

Your team will not draw this line for you

Two things have to happen for a team to hold the line on its own. Neither will.

Someone has to notice when work crossed it. They will not. In a randomized trial, experienced developers using AI finished 19% slower and then estimated they had gone 20% faster. Thirty-nine points of error, from the people closest to the work. Microsoft's research names the mechanism: the more someone trusts an AI output, the less they scrutinize it. Your strongest engineers are the least reliable narrators you have on this.

Then someone has to say it out loud. Most companies are already broken here.

Three years from a senior engineer's chair: layoffs, two reorgs, a roadmap rewritten twice after she was told it was locked. Then leadership walks in with an AI mandate and asks for enthusiasm.

She complies. Of course she complies.

When you strip people of the ability to question, to learn at their own pace, to experiment with tools on their own terms, you strip them of agency. John Cutler, The Beautiful Mess, June 2026

Compliance and adoption produce identical slides. Underneath they are opposites. Adoption means your team tells you where the tool hurts. Compliance means they use it there anyway, because usage is what you measure.

So a product manager holding a judged outcome and an autonomous agent has a choice. Say the quiet part, that you can hit the date or know whether it worked but not both. Or ship it and move on.

Guess which one she picks when the mandate came from her skip level.

Read skepticism as signal, not as a mindset problem. When an engineer says the output is not good enough for this particular work, that is information about the work. Most organizations have trained the reflex to hear it as information about the engineer.

DORA surveyed nearly 5,000 practitioners last year and found AI adoption tracks positively with throughput and negatively with delivery stability. AI does not fix a team, it amplifies what is already there. Which is why the line gets written down by you, rather than discovered by them.

The rule is really about permission

A product manager who says "this outcome is judged, so the agent assists here" is resisting the initiative if nothing is written down. She is executing policy if something is. Same sentence. Different career risk.

That is the value. Not the grid. The permission.

Cutler asks leaders to say who benefits from an adoption push. Usually some mix of cost, speed, and a board slide. Your teams already assume it. Saying it out loud costs you nothing.

This fails when leadership publishes the framework and still asks only about velocity every week. People read the meeting, not the document.

Take this into your next review

Three questions for your next review

  1. What benefit is the user supposed to realize, and is that a number or a judgment?
  2. If it is a judgment, who is doing the judging and on what date?
  3. What did we hand the agent as a definition of done, and could a machine check it?

Stop asking whether your teams use AI enough. They do. Ask what benefit your user is supposed to realize, how you would know they got it, and who is on the calendar to check. If nobody is, you know how this ends.
